1Executive Summary

Open section 1

USACE programs are organized ways of delivering engineering, water-resources, regulatory, emergency, military, and technical-support work. An authority is the law or delegated legal power that allows a particular kind of work; a program is the managed set of activities USACE carries out under one or more authorities.

Most important idea: A sound request begins by matching the problem to the correct authority, then checking the separate requirements for funding, partnership, environmental compliance, approvals, and execution. “USACE can help” does not itself mean that USACE may build, permit, fund, or alter something.

USACE authorities commonly appear in Civil Works water-resources delivery, the Regulatory Program, emergency flood and coastal assistance, military programs, and reimbursable technical services. In many Civil Works cases, Congress authorizes a study or project and separately appropriates money; non-Federal sponsors often have defined roles and cost-sharing responsibilities. Continuing Authorities Programs (CAP) are a major exception: they allow certain limited-scope project types to proceed without a separate project-specific congressional authorization, subject to the governing statute, limits, policy, and available funds.

Established framework This resource uses official USACE publications and program pages. It is a learning aid, not a determination of legal authority, eligibility, cost share, permit need, or funding availability for any particular request.

2The Core Idea — Explain It Simply

Open section 2

Think of authority as permission and program as the delivery system

Suppose a community says, “Our river is damaging a road.” That statement describes a problem, not a solution and not a legal pathway. Before USACE can act, staff must ask: Is the requested outcome planning advice, an emergency response, a small flood-risk project, a modification to an existing federal project, a permit decision, or a larger federally authorized project?

Plain-language definition: An authority says what USACE is allowed to do and under what conditions. A program supplies the people, process, policy, and funding structure to do it.

A useful analogy — building permits and public works

A city may have a building code that permits a kind of construction, a capital-improvement program that manages projects, and a yearly budget that pays for selected work. USACE works similarly, but federal statutes, appropriations, delegated authorities, and formal agreements are central. The analogy helps separate permission from delivery; it breaks down because federal water-resources decisions can also require congressional action, statutory cost sharing, environmental review, and multiple agency consultations.

Simple real-world pattern

A county needs technical floodplain information to inform land-use decisions. It may be a better fit for the Floodplain Management Services (FPMS) program—authorized by Section 206 of the Flood Control Act of 1960—than for a construction project. FPMS can provide technical services and planning guidance within program funding limits; it is not a blank check to construct a levee. See Source 6.

Central mechanism: problem → authority pathway → eligibility and partnership checks → funding/agreements → planning, design, permitting or execution → long-term responsibilities. Each arrow can stop or change the effort.

3Progressive Levels of Understanding

Open section 3
Level 1 — Beginner: What is it?
Basic definitionEstablished framework
USACE
A federal engineering organization with several mission areas. For this topic, focus on its Civil Works, Regulatory, emergency, Military Programs, and reimbursable-support roles.
Authority
A statute or lawful delegation that establishes what action may be taken, by whom, and often for what purpose.
Program
An organized way to administer work under authorities—for example, a program for small projects, technical assistance, permits, or military construction support.
Simple example
Section 10 of the Rivers and Harbors Act is an authority behind certain USACE permit decisions affecting navigable waters; the Regulatory Program administers that work.
Key takeaway
Do not begin with a favored solution. Begin with the problem and determine the applicable pathway.
Level 2 — Foundational: How does it work?
ProcessEstablished framework

Common pathways

1
Civil Works project delivery

A study evaluates alternatives. A recommended project generally needs congressional authorization and appropriations before construction, then transitions to operations and maintenance.

2
Continuing authorities

CAP covers defined, limited-scope water-resources project types under standing statutory authorities, instead of a separate project-specific authorization.

3
Assistance / approval

Other pathways provide planning advice, permits, emergency assistance, or permission to alter an existing USACE Civil Works project.

Important distinctions

Authorization ≠ appropriation
Authorization establishes or permits an activity; appropriations provide budget authority. Both may matter, and available funds still affect timing.
Study ≠ construction
Planning can define a problem and compare alternatives without committing USACE to build a project.
Permit ≠ project sponsorship
A Regulatory Program permit decision is an evaluation of a proposed activity under permit authorities; it is not USACE adopting the applicant’s project as a Civil Works project.
Section 408 ≠ permit
A Section 408 permission concerns an alteration, occupation, or use of an existing USACE Civil Works project. Other federal, state, Tribal, and local approvals may still be needed.
Common misconception: “A local sponsor asks, so USACE must act.” A request can start discussion, but statutes, eligibility, public interest, policy, funding, technical feasibility, environmental requirements, and agreements shape what can proceed.
Level 3 — Intermediate: Why does it work this way?
Governance and tradeoffsEstablished framework

USACE authorities divide federal work into purpose-specific lanes so that public spending, project risks, environmental effects, sponsor roles, and congressional oversight can be handled transparently. The planning process tests alternatives rather than assuming that a structural project is the answer. Current Civil Works planning policy addresses navigation, flood risk management, coastal storm risk management, aquatic ecosystem restoration, hydropower, and other topics through a structured planning framework. See Source 2.

Problem framing
Staff identify problems, opportunities, objectives, and constraints before comparing alternatives.
Federal interest
The work must fit a federal authority and program purpose, not merely a desirable local outcome.
Risk and lifecycle thinking
Projects are judged across planning, design, construction, operations, maintenance, safety, resilience, and environmental effects—not only first cost.
Partnership
Non-Federal sponsors commonly provide local knowledge, lands/easements, funding or in-kind work as allowed, and post-construction roles defined in agreements.

More realistic example: A navigation channel needs periodic dredging. A proposal to use dredged material beneficially may involve the authorized federal navigation project, environmental review and permitting, sponsor coordination, funding rules, and possibly other authorities. The fact that a project has a navigation purpose does not decide every placement or cost-sharing question.

Teaching-model limit: This resource groups pathways for learning. Actual authorities overlap, change through legislation and policy, and may be implemented differently based on location, project history, delegated decision level, and the facts of a request.
Level 4 — Advanced: What are the deeper implications?
Boundary conditionsContext-dependent

Authorities often interact, but one does not replace another

A single action can trigger multiple legal and procedural lanes. For example, a proposal near a federal levee might need Section 408 permission because it affects a USACE Civil Works project, a Regulatory Program permit if it involves regulated waters or structures, environmental compliance, real-estate authorization, and approvals from the project operator or other jurisdictions. The correct sequence and decision authority are fact-specific.

Competing public values are built into the work

Water-resources decisions can require tradeoffs among life safety, navigation reliability, habitat, recreation, cultural resources, cost, equity, resilience, and future uncertainty. The central analytical question is not only “Can this be built?” but also “What authorized purpose does it serve, compared with what alternatives, at what lifecycle and residual risk?”

Failure modes

  • Starting design before identifying the actual authority or sponsor.
  • Treating a planning product as authorization to build.
  • Assuming an old project authorization covers a new use or modification.
  • Ignoring operations, maintenance, real estate, environmental commitments, or residual risk.
  • Using outdated dollar caps, policy citations, or jurisdictional assumptions without current verification.
Level 5 — Expert Perspective: How do specialists think about it?
Professional lensContext-dependent
Authority map
Specialists build a traceable map from the stated problem to statutes, project documents, regulations, delegated authorities, policies, appropriations, and agreements.
Decision rights
They identify who can recommend, approve, fund, permit, operate, or accept residual responsibilities. Technical feasibility alone is not decision authority.
Portfolio lens
They assess how a proposed action affects systems, other projects, budget priorities, capacity, and lifecycle performance.
Documentation discipline
They distinguish verified authority from an interpretation, and flag open legal, environmental, real-estate, tribal-consultation, and budget questions early.
Mastery
Mastery means knowing when to seek current district, division, headquarters, counsel, planning, regulatory, real-estate, or resource-agency review—not memorizing section numbers.

6Worked Examples

Open section 6
Example 1 — Hypothetical: A county wants flood information
Illustrative examplePlanning assistance
Situation
A county is revising a comprehensive plan and asks for flood-depth, flood-frequency, and flood-warning planning information.
Relevant facts / assumptions
The request is for technical information and planning support, not a commitment to design or build a project. The county is an eligible local governmental partner.
Step 1 — frame the need
The primary need is improved floodplain decision-making.
Step 2 — match pathway
FPMS is a plausible starting point because it provides flood-hazard information, technical services, and planning guidance under Section 206 of the Flood Control Act of 1960, within program limits.
Step 3 — verify conditions
Confirm district procedures, funding availability, the written request, scope, existing data, schedule, and whether another assistance program better fits.
Result
The likely product is information or a planning study, not a federal commitment to construct flood-risk infrastructure.
Key lesson
Match the requested action to the authority; do not treat a planning program as a construction program.
Limitations
Actual availability and scope depend on current program guidance, workload, funding, geography, and district review.
Example 2 — Hypothetical: A small town seeks a flood-risk project
Illustrative exampleCAP screening
Situation
A town experiences repeated overland flooding and asks USACE to consider a floodwall, elevation, or other measures.
Relevant facts / assumptions
No existing project authority is identified. The town can identify a potential non-Federal sponsor. The proposed scope may be limited, but that has not been verified.
Step 1 — avoid solution lock-in
State the flood-risk problem, assets at risk, objectives, constraints, and nonstructural as well as structural alternatives.
Step 2 — screen CAP
Section 205 small flood-risk management authority is a plausible pathway to examine. CAP authorities have defined purposes, project limits, funding constraints, and cost-share provisions.
Step 3 — test fit
USACE and the sponsor assess technical feasibility, economics, environmental acceptability, real estate, sponsor responsibilities, cost sharing, and current program limits.
Result
If the project fits and is selected, CAP may provide a route without a separate project-specific congressional construction authorization. If it does not fit, a larger Civil Works study or another local/state pathway may be more appropriate.
Key lesson
CAP is a standing authority for specified small-project categories, not a general shortcut around analysis or resource constraints.
Alternative outcome
Nonstructural measures, local projects, hazard-mitigation programs of other agencies, or a wider watershed study may better address the problem.
Example 3 — Hypothetical: A utility proposes work through a federal levee
Illustrative exampleMultiple authorities
Situation
A utility wants to install a buried transmission line across a levee that is part of an existing USACE Civil Works project.
Relevant facts / assumptions
The levee is federally authorized and USACE has an interest in its usefulness. The proposal may involve construction, access, easements, and water crossings.
Step 1 — identify the federal asset
Confirm project status, ownership/operation, existing easements, and the exact feature that would be affected.
Step 2 — identify the principal review
Section 408 is likely relevant because it authorizes permission for use, occupation, or alteration of a USACE Civil Works project when the required public-interest and project-usefulness findings are met.
Step 3 — identify parallel lanes
Assess whether Regulatory permits, real-estate rights, environmental compliance, utility approvals, or state/local permits are also required.
Result
Section 408 permission, if granted, is only one decision. It does not itself grant all property rights or supersede other laws.
Key lesson
Different authorities can apply to one physical action because each governs a different public interest.
Limitations
Decision authority, required documentation, and review sequence depend on the project, location, risk, and current guidance.
Example 4 — Hypothetical: Flood threat before landfall
Illustrative exampleEmergency support
Situation
A state emergency-management organization expects river flooding from a major storm and requests USACE technical support and possible flood-fighting assistance.
Relevant facts / assumptions
The threat is immediate, local/state/Tribal resources are engaged, and the work would be temporary response support rather than routine construction.
Step 1 — distinguish emergency from project delivery
The immediate objective is protection during an active or imminent event, not long-term project formulation.
Step 2 — assess PL 84-99
Public Law 84-99 is USACE’s basic authority for specified emergency activities before, during, and after qualifying flood and coastal events, funded through Flood Control and Coastal Emergencies appropriations.
Step 3 — coordinate conditions
USACE assistance supplements—not replaces—state, Tribal, and local efforts. Requests, thresholds, eligibility, available resources, and approvals are determined in real time under current procedures.
Result
Emergency assistance can address an immediate threat; it is not the authority for permanent flood-risk reduction construction.
Key lesson
Time horizon matters: emergency authorities and long-term Civil Works authorities solve different parts of the same flood problem.
Limitations
Operational conditions and emergency eligibility require current coordination with the responsible USACE emergency-management office.

9Related Concepts and Connections

Open section 9
Water Resources Development Acts (WRDAs)
Prerequisite / source of authority
WRDAs are a recurring vehicle through which Congress authorizes USACE studies, projects, programs, and policy changes.
Why it matters: Many Civil Works authorities and project authorizations trace to WRDA provisions, but the exact statute and amendment history must be verified.
Federal budgeting
Consequence
Authorization and appropriation are distinct actions within different decision processes.
Why it matters: It explains why eligibility or authorization does not guarantee execution in a particular fiscal year.
Environmental compliance and consultation
Application
Environmental statutes and consultation requirements can shape alternatives, mitigation, timing, and permit conditions.
Why it matters: An authority to consider an action does not waive cross-cutting environmental responsibilities.
Risk management and resilience
Extension
Flood and coastal work often evaluates probabilities, consequences, residual risk, and lifecycle performance.
Why it matters: The best pathway is not automatically the one that produces the largest structure.
Real estate and asset stewardship
Contrast
Use of federal land or alteration of federal assets has separate property, safety, and stewardship questions.
Why it matters: A permit, a project agreement, and a Section 408 permission can each address different issues.
Military Programs and IIS
Application
USACE also supports military construction, real estate, installation readiness, environmental work, and reimbursable customers.
Why it matters: “USACE program” is broader than Civil Works; funding source and customer relationship may differ substantially.

10Common Misunderstandings

Open section 10
“Authority and funding are the same thing.”

Correction: They are different. An authority may permit or establish work; an appropriation provides budget authority. Why it matters: A project can be authorized but await appropriations, and available funds cannot be assumed to create a new authority. Counterexample: A planning authority may exist, but no current work can start until the required funding and agreements are in place.

“A CAP project needs no congressional involvement.”

Correction: CAP uses standing authority enacted by Congress. It may avoid a separate project-specific authorization, but it remains subject to statutory purpose, limits, policy, funding, partnership, and review requirements. Why it matters: CAP is not unrestricted discretionary construction authority.

“A USACE permit means USACE will construct or maintain the work.”

Correction: A Department of the Army permit is a regulatory decision on an applicant’s proposed activity. Why it matters: The applicant, not USACE Civil Works, normally remains responsible for the project. Counterexample: A permitted pier is not automatically a federally maintained navigation feature.

“Section 408 is just another name for a Regulatory permit.”

Correction: Section 408 concerns a proposed alteration, occupation, or use of a USACE Civil Works project; the Regulatory Program administers different permit authorities. Why it matters: One proposal can need both reviews, plus other approvals.

“Emergency assistance solves the long-term problem.”

Correction: PL 84-99 support addresses qualifying emergency conditions and is generally temporary. Why it matters: Long-term risk reduction normally requires a separate planning, funding, and implementation pathway.

Introductory simplification, not an error: Calling USACE “the federal water-resources agency” is a useful orientation. In practice, USACE works alongside many federal, state, Tribal, local, and regional partners with distinct legal roles.

11Practical Applications

Open section 11

Everyday life and communities

Situation: Residents ask why a floodwall, beach project, reservoir, navigation channel, or wetland-restoration effort moves slowly. How this helps: The authority/program model explains that planning, funding, sponsorship, environmental review, engineering, and long-term operation are separate workstreams. Benefit: More realistic public expectations. Limitation: It does not answer whether a particular project is justified or funded.

Professional project development

Situation: A local government or installation identifies an infrastructure or water-resources problem. How this helps: Staff can prepare a problem statement, identify the asset and location, distinguish urgent from long-term needs, and request the appropriate initial conversation rather than submitting a solution-only request. Benefit: Better scoping and fewer avoidable handoffs. Risk: Do not represent a preliminary pathway screen as an official eligibility determination.

Organizations and decision-making

Situation: Leaders compare alternatives—local construction, USACE assistance, a federal Civil Works project, or a regulatory application. How this helps: It separates decision criteria: statutory fit, public purpose, sponsor capacity, lifecycle commitments, funding, timing, residual risk, and environmental impacts. Benefit: Decision memoranda can show assumptions and dependencies. Tradeoff: The most legally available option may not be the fastest, cheapest, or most appropriate.

Military and interagency work

Situation: A federal customer seeks engineering, construction, real-estate, environmental, or technical support. How this helps: Military Programs and IIS illustrate that USACE can operate as a provider of technical services under assignments and reimbursable arrangements, not only as a Civil Works project sponsor. Benefit: Better alignment of customer need, funding source, scope, and agreements. Limitation: Specific support authorities and agreement requirements require program and counsel review.

Evidence-supported practice: Start a request with the problem, location, affected assets, desired outcome, urgency, known partners, and constraints. Then ask the appropriate USACE office which authority/program screen is appropriate. This is a practical triage recommendation, not a substitute for official intake procedures.

13Final Summary

Open section 13

Central idea: USACE programs deliver work, while USACE authorities establish the legal lanes and conditions within which that work may occur.

  • Start with the problem and desired outcome—not an assumed project or section number.
  • Separate authorization, appropriation, program selection, and approval; each answers a different question.
  • CAP provides standing authority for certain limited-scope project types, but it still requires statutory fit, review, partnerships, and available resources.
  • Regulatory permits, Section 408 permissions, Civil Works project delivery, and emergency assistance are distinct pathways that may overlap.
  • Non-Federal sponsors and other partners often have central roles in requests, cost sharing, real estate, operations, maintenance, and local coordination.
  • Specialists document assumptions, trace decisions to current authorities and guidance, and seek qualified review before making commitments.
Next learning step: Choose one local or professional scenario and create a one-page “authority map”: problem, location, proposed action, affected federal asset (if any), likely pathway, potential sponsor, funding question, approvals, and open questions. Then validate it with the responsible USACE district/program office and counsel as appropriate.
If you remember only one thing: The right question is not “Which USACE program do I want?” It is “What authority, conditions, and delivery pathway fit this problem—and what else must be true before action can occur?”
