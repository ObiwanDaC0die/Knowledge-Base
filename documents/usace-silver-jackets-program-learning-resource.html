1Executive Summary

Silver Jackets is a U.S. Army Corps of Engineers (USACE) initiative that supports state-led, interagency teams working on priorities for flood-risk management. Rather than being a response force or a grant program with one standard project, each team is a standing forum that connects organizations with different authorities, information, programs, and resources. Teams may work on activities such as hazard-mitigation planning, inundation mapping, exercises, emergency-action planning, risk communication, and post-event data coordination. The program matters because flood risk crosses jurisdictional and organizational boundaries that no single agency can usually address alone. Indispensable takeaway: Silver Jackets is chiefly a coordination mechanism for turning a state’s flood-risk priorities into more integrated, complementary action.

Assumption: This resource provides a national, introductory view. The membership, charter, projects, and operating practices of a particular state or territory team can differ; consult that team’s current materials for local details.

2The Core Idea — Explain It Simply

Flooding is not just a water problem. It can involve forecasting, mapping, land use, infrastructure, emergency operations, insurance, public communication, environmental management, recovery funding, and community decisions. Silver Jackets gives organizations a recurring place to connect those pieces.

Plain-language definition: A Silver Jackets team is a state-led group of agencies and partners that works together on flood-risk priorities and, in some cases, related natural-hazard issues.

Analogy: Think of a community flood challenge as a complicated repair that requires several trades. One person may understand the electrical system, another the plumbing, another permits, and another the building’s occupants. Silver Jackets is the coordination table where those specialists agree on the problem, identify gaps, and sequence compatible work. The analogy breaks down because public agencies retain their own legal authorities, budgets, and decision processes; a team cannot simply direct all members as one contractor.

Concrete illustration: A state may identify that communities need clearer information about areas that could flood. One partner may contribute hydrologic or mapping expertise, another may connect the work to hazard-mitigation planning, another may support public messaging, and local officials may explain what residents need to decide. The useful output is not merely a map: it is a shared path for using the information.

Central mechanism: state priorities + recurring interagency coordination + each participant’s existing authorities and resources → more coherent flood-risk-reduction work, with fewer avoidable gaps or duplicative efforts.

3Progressive Levels of Understanding

Beginner — What is it for?
OrientationOfficial program description
Definition
Silver Jackets teams are interagency teams that facilitate collaborative solutions to state flood-risk priorities.
Purpose
They help partners work continuously across the flood-risk-management life cycle instead of meeting only after a disaster.
Example
A team convenes agencies to align a tabletop exercise, flood-risk information, and community outreach around a state-identified need.
Takeaway
The team is a forum for coordination and shared problem-solving, not a replacement for any member agency.
Foundational — What parts make the program work?
Key distinctions
State-led priorities
The state or territory determines its team’s priorities; local context shapes what work is useful.
Participants
Common participants include state agencies and federal partners such as USACE, the Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA), National Weather Service (NWS), U.S. Geological Survey (USGS), and Natural Resources Conservation Service (NRCS). Tribes, local governments, academia, nonprofits, and others may participate.
Mechanism
Regular communication reveals overlapping programs, unmet needs, data limitations, and practical opportunities to combine effort.
Misconception to avoid
“Interagency” does not mean that authority, funding, or accountability is pooled automatically. Participants still act within their own authorities and available resources.
Intermediate — How does coordination change decisions?
Application and tradeoffs
Life-cycle thinking
Teams can connect preparedness, mitigation, response, and recovery so that lessons or information from one phase improve the next.
Variables
Useful work depends on the flood problem, state priorities, participating organizations, data availability, timing, community capacity, authorities, and funding.
Realistic case pattern
After repeated flooding, partners may compare what information exists, identify a communication gap, choose a technical or planning product, and connect that product to agencies that can use it.
Tradeoff
Broad participation can improve perspective and leverage, but it takes time to establish common language, schedules, and a feasible scope.
Advanced — What are the boundaries and uncertainties?
Context-dependent
Governance boundary
Silver Jackets is a collaborative mechanism, not a universal governance authority. A team’s influence depends on voluntary participation, relationships, decisions by responsible officials, and the authorities of member organizations.
Risk boundary
Flood-risk reduction does not eliminate all flood risk. Maps, models, forecasts, and plans inform decisions but contain assumptions and uncertainty.
Different interpretations
Partners may prioritize technical analysis, planning integration, public communication, or project implementation differently. Productive teams make those differences visible and seek complementary roles rather than assuming one metric decides everything.
Edge case
Some team activities address related hazards such as drought or wildfire when that supports state priorities, but flooding remains the central program focus.
Expert Perspective — What does mastery look like?
Professional practice
Framework
Frame work around a specific state priority, a shared problem statement, the risk-management life cycle, participating authorities, an implementable output, and named users of that output.
Decision quality
Strong coordination distinguishes who can decide, who can fund, who can provide technical support, who must be consulted, and how a result will be maintained.
Adjacent disciplines
Floodplain management, emergency management, hydrology, engineering, land-use planning, public communication, environmental stewardship, and social vulnerability analysis all may matter.
Sign of mastery
You can explain not only what a product is, but why it is needed, who will use it, what assumptions constrain it, and what follow-on action is required.

5Interactive or Dynamic Learning Elements

Use the prediction exercise below to practice identifying when a problem needs coordination rather than a single technical answer. It works without JavaScript because each reveal is a native disclosure.

Prediction / reveal: A community wants “a flood solution.” What should the team ask first?
Think before revealing

Scenario: A county reports repeated road closures and asks partners to “fix flooding.” Before choosing a product or project, predict three questions that would make the request actionable.

Reveal a strong starting set of questions
  • Define the consequence: Which roads, populations, facilities, or functions are affected, and during what kind of event?
  • Identify existing information: What mapping, observations, plans, forecasts, and prior studies already exist—and what decisions do they support?
  • Clarify users and authorities: Who can act on the result, who owns or operates the affected assets, and what authorities or programs could support next steps?

Why this changes the answer: “Flooding” may describe different problems: warning time, evacuation routes, drainage, development patterns, infrastructure vulnerability, or public understanding. A coordination forum helps distinguish them before duplicating work.

Limit: Asking good questions does not guarantee funding, authority, or agreement on a solution.

Mini-quiz: Is this a Silver Jackets coordination opportunity?

Choose mentally before opening the feedback: A state wants to make flood-risk information easier for local officials and residents to use. The relevant data exist in several agencies, the state hazard-mitigation office is updating planning guidance, and local officials need plain-language communication. Is this likely a coordination opportunity?

Feedback

Yes—likely. The scenario has a state-defined need, multiple information holders and users, and an implementation connection to planning and communication. A Silver Jackets team could help align the problem statement, data roles, a usable product, and the organizations that can carry the work forward.

Do not overread this conclusion: it does not establish that the team must lead, that a project is approved, or that the information is sufficient for any regulatory or engineering decision.

6Worked Examples

Example 1 — Aligning a flood-risk communication workshop
Hypothetical, introductory
Situation
Residents receive technical flood information but do not understand what actions it implies.
Inputs and assumptions
Hypothetical: a state emergency-management office, USACE district staff, NWS staff, and local floodplain managers can participate; no new funding is assumed.
Stepwise application
1) Define the audience and decisions they face. 2) Inventory current messages and tools. 3) Identify contradictory terms or missing explanations. 4) Convene a workshop that explains risk, uncertainty, and protective actions consistently. 5) Capture follow-on owners for materials and outreach.
Result
A shared communication approach and a clearer handoff to organizations that own ongoing outreach.
Key lesson
Coordination can create value by making existing expertise and messages more usable together.
Limitations / alternatives
A workshop is not proof that risk understanding or behavior changed. Evaluation, accessibility review, and local outreach capacity may still be needed.
Example 2 — Preparing a tabletop exercise around a levee-related concern
Hypothetical, intermediate
Situation
A state wants to improve coordination for a potential flood event affecting a community near a levee.
Inputs and assumptions
Hypothetical: participants have existing emergency plans and technical information, but have not recently exercised roles together.
Stepwise application
1) Agree on a scenario and the decisions to test. 2) Identify representatives from emergency management, floodplain management, weather/forecasting, infrastructure, and local government. 3) Walk through information sharing, warning, evacuation, and documentation decisions. 4) Record gaps, dependencies, and corrective actions. 5) Assign owners and review progress later.
Result
An exercise-based list of realistic coordination improvements rather than a generic emergency plan.
Key lesson
Standing relationships make it easier to discover coordination gaps before an actual event.
Limitations / alternatives
An exercise does not validate all real-world capabilities. Field drills, plan updates, and decision-maker review may be required.
Example 3 — Making inundation information decision-relevant
Hypothetical, advanced
Situation
Several communities need flood-inundation information, but they differ in data quality, mapping needs, and capacity to use the information.
Inputs and assumptions
Hypothetical: some technical data exist; participants must operate within separate agency authorities and resource constraints.
Stepwise application
1) Define intended uses—emergency routing, public awareness, planning, or another purpose. 2) Identify the technical assumptions and uncertainty that users need to understand. 3) Prioritize a feasible pilot area with committed users. 4) Determine which partner can supply or maintain each input. 5) Pair the information product with guidance, training, and a maintenance plan.
Result
A scoped, user-oriented effort that links technical information to decisions rather than treating a map as the endpoint.
Key lesson
The best technical product depends on the decision it must support and the capacity to sustain it.
Limitations / alternatives
Inundation information is not a guarantee of conditions at a specific time or location. Qualified technical review is needed for operational, engineering, or regulatory use.

9Related Concepts and Connections

Shared responsibility
Connection

Flood risk is shaped by many decisions and conditions. The program’s collaborative model reflects that no agency—or community member—can usually manage all parts alone.

Integrated risk management
Connection

Technical, planning, communication, and response work are stronger when they are designed to reinforce each other across the life cycle.

Whole-community practice
Connection

Local governments, Tribes, communities, academia, nonprofits, and other partners may bring essential context and implementation capacity, though participation varies.

Boundary-spanning
Connection

Silver Jackets crosses organizational boundaries by linking people who use different vocabularies, data, timelines, and authorities. This is a coordination skill as much as a technical one.

Why these connections matter

A technically sound flood product can still fail to reduce risk if it is not connected to a decision, a responsible user, understandable communication, or a feasible implementation path. Conversely, a well-coordinated team cannot substitute for sound technical analysis, legal authorities, or community consent. Effective practice needs both coordination and discipline-specific expertise.

10Common Misunderstandings

Misconceptions and corrections
Likely misconceptionAccurate correctionWhy the difference mattersCounterexample or qualifier
“Silver Jackets is a USACE response team.”USACE supports the initiative, but the teams are state-led, interagency forums focused on collaborative solutions.It prevents confusion between coordination and a deployable response organization.A team may support preparedness or recovery coordination without conducting response operations itself.
“Every state team has the same members and projects.”Teams are unique and prioritize state or territory needs; participation and focal areas vary.It avoids copying another state’s model without considering local conditions.Some teams include Tribal, academic, local, or nonprofit participation; others may differ.
“A map is the solution.”A map is information. It becomes useful only when linked to an intended decision, users, communication, and maintenance.It focuses attention on outcomes rather than products alone.Communities may need training or planning changes as much as a new technical layer.
“Coordination removes funding and authority limits.”Participants continue to act within their own authorities, budgets, and processes.It sets realistic expectations and supports credible scoping.A team can identify a funding path, but it does not automatically award funds.
“Flood risk reduction means zero risk.”Risk can often be reduced or better managed, but residual risk and uncertainty remain.It supports honest communication and sound decisions.Even a well-prepared community can face flooding beyond a plan’s assumptions.

A harmless introductory simplification is to call Silver Jackets “a partnership.” The fuller explanation is that it is a state-led, ongoing interagency coordination mechanism with locally variable membership and activities.

11Practical Applications

State and local planning
Planning application

Situation: Agencies are updating hazard-mitigation or floodplain-management plans. How this informs action: the team can compare priorities, data, program timelines, and implementation responsibilities. Potential benefit: fewer disconnected plans and clearer use of existing resources. Risks and tradeoffs: coordination can slow early scoping if roles are unclear; planning still requires responsible agency review and public/Tribal engagement as applicable. Evidence status: USACE lists state hazard-mitigation-plan updates among team activities, but outcomes vary by project and state.

Emergency preparedness and exercises
Preparedness application

Situation: Partners need to test how information and responsibilities connect before a flood. How this informs action: a tabletop exercise can examine decision points, information flow, and unresolved dependencies. Potential benefit: improved shared understanding and a practical corrective-action list. Risks and tradeoffs: an exercise may create false confidence if actions are not tracked, funded, and retested. Evidence status: USACE identifies tabletop exercises and emergency-action-plan development as examples of activities.

Technical information and public communication
Information-to-action application

Situation: Technical flood information is available but underused. How this informs action: partners can identify who needs the information, what decisions it supports, how uncertainty should be communicated, and who maintains the product. Potential benefit: more usable, consistent risk communication. Risks and ethics: oversimplifying uncertainty, excluding communities with accessibility or language needs, or using information outside its intended purpose can cause harm. Evidence status: risk-communication workshops and inundation mapping are listed activities; no universal effectiveness claim is made here.

Professional-use boundary: This resource is educational. It does not determine flood hazards, approve a project, interpret a regulation, establish an emergency procedure, or substitute for qualified technical, legal, emergency-management, or community decision-making review.

12Knowledge Check

  1. In one sentence, what is the core purpose of a Silver Jackets team?
  2. Why is “state-led” an important part of the program’s design?
  3. Name two kinds of organizations that may participate and explain why different roles are useful.
  4. How is an interagency coordination forum different from a single agency’s response unit?
  5. Why should a team define intended users and decisions before developing a technical product?
  6. Application: A state has good flood data but weak local use of it. Outline a three-step coordination approach that does not assume new funding.
  7. Analysis: A proposed activity is popular but has no identified owner, user, authority, or maintenance plan. What concerns should the team raise?
Answer Key
Model answers; reasonable alternatives accepted
  1. Core purpose: A Silver Jackets team helps partners collaboratively address state flood-risk priorities by connecting their knowledge, programs, resources, and roles.
  2. State-led: The state or territory sets priorities, helping ensure that the team focuses on locally relevant needs rather than imposing a one-size-fits-all agenda.
  3. Participants: For example, a state hazard-mitigation office can connect work to statewide planning; USACE can provide relevant technical or program expertise; FEMA, NWS, USGS, NRCS, local governments, Tribes, and others may contribute different information or implementation pathways. Any two well-explained roles are acceptable.
  4. Difference from a response unit: The forum coordinates planning and problem-solving across organizations and phases. It does not itself replace the operational authorities, command structures, or responsibilities of participating agencies.
  5. Users and decisions: A product has value only if someone can interpret and use it for a defined decision. Identifying this early helps set suitable scope, assumptions, format, communication, and maintenance responsibility.
  6. Application model: (1) Convene data holders, local users, and planning/communication partners to define the use gap. (2) Inventory existing materials and choose a simple priority use case, such as local planning or public outreach. (3) Assign owners for translation, dissemination, and follow-up training. Alternatives that establish a clear problem, link available resources, and create accountable follow-through are acceptable.
  7. Analysis model: Raise feasibility and sustainability concerns: Who needs the outcome? Who is authorized to act? Who will maintain it? What resources and approvals are required? The team should either identify these elements or rescope the activity. Popularity alone does not make a project implementable.

13Final Summary

Central idea: The USACE Silver Jackets Program supports state-led teams that bring organizations together to turn shared flood-risk priorities into more coordinated, practical work.

  • Silver Jackets is a standing interagency coordination mechanism, not a single operational force or universal funding source.
  • The state or territory sets priorities, so team membership and activities differ by location.
  • Teams can support work throughout preparedness, mitigation, response, and recovery.
  • Useful coordination starts with a defined problem, intended users, available information, roles, authorities, and follow-on actions.
  • Technical products such as maps matter most when they are connected to decisions, communication, and maintenance.
  • Coordination improves alignment but does not remove legal, budget, technical, or community-engagement requirements.

Next learning step: Locate the current Silver Jackets page or charter for the state or territory of interest and compare its stated priorities, members, and recent activities with the framework in this resource.

If you remember only one thing: Silver Jackets creates a recurring “many agencies, one solution” forum so that flood-risk work is more connected to the real decisions and partners needed to make it useful.
